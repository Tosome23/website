Lithium Battery Air Freight From Korea
UN3480 vs UN3481, PI/PII/Wh Calculation, PAX vs CAO

Every airline applies different rules. That's why shipments get rejected. Lithium batteries are one of the trickiest items in international logistics. EMS won't handle them as a rule, and if you can't tell PI from PII, you can't ship at all. Here's the UN numbers, PI/PII/Wh calculation, the difference between passenger (PAX) and cargo-only (CAO) aircraft, and the 2026 updates — organized under IATA DGR rules for exporters shipping from Korea.

1. Why Exporting Lithium Batteries From Korea Is So Difficult

Lithium batteries are designated dangerous goods (Class 9) by the International Air Transport Association (IATA). Because of the risk of overheating, fire, and explosion, each airline sets its own rules for accepting them. Even major couriers (DHL, FedEx, UPS) cannot accept them without prior dangerous goods declaration.

EMS (international postal service) refuses standalone lithium battery shipments as a rule, and some countries ban them from import entirely. Shipping without understanding the regulations often results in the cargo being returned or seized by customs at the destination.

⚠️ IATA regulations are revised every year. This guide reflects the 2026 rules, but airlines and destination countries may apply additional restrictions. Always confirm with a specialist forwarder before shipping.

2. What Changed in the 2026 IATA Lithium Battery Air Rules

Under the 2026 IATA DGR 67th edition, the following changes were made or tightened. Shipments prepared under the old rules are increasingly being rejected at acceptance, so review this carefully.

①
Tighter State of Charge (SOC) limit
Cargo lithium-ion cells and batteries may only be loaded with a state of charge of 30% or below. This is stricter than the previous limit of 50% or below.
②
More items banned from passenger aircraft (PAX) cargo holds
Standalone lithium-ion cells and certain grades of lithium-metal batteries are now banned from passenger aircraft (PAX) cargo holds. More shipments now require Cargo Aircraft Only (CAO) transport.
③
More precise PI/PII classification requirements
The Wh (watt-hour) thresholds for Section I (PI) and Section II (PII) have been broken down further. PI is limited to cargo-only aircraft (CAO); PII can fly on both passenger (PAX) and cargo aircraft — a misclassification results in immediate rejection.
④
Tighter packing inspection and marking rules
The requirement to use UN-certified packaging, orientation arrow labels, and handling label placement rules have all been made more specific.

3. PI vs PII — the Wh Threshold Table

The first thing to check for lithium battery air freight is whether the battery falls under PI (Section I) or PII (Section II). This depends on the Wh (watt-hour) capacity and the battery form (cell vs. pack).

Lithium-ion battery pack100Wh or belowAbove 100Wh - 160WhAbove 160Wh

💡 Wh calculation: Wh = voltage (V) × capacity (Ah). Example: 3.7V × 2.5Ah = 9.25Wh, 11.1V × 5Ah = 55.5Wh (typical for a laptop battery). A single 18650 cell is usually around 10-12Wh.

PAX (Passenger Aircraft) and CAO (Cargo Aircraft Only) refer to the type of flight a lithium battery can be loaded onto. If PII conditions are met, both PAX and CAO can carry the shipment; PI-classified batteries must go by CAO only, due to fire risk. Battery loading rules on passenger aircraft are far stricter for fire-safety reasons, and CAO availability varies by airline and route, so always confirm before shipping.

6. Packing Requirements for Lithium Batteries

Packing standards for air-freighted lithium batteries are set out in the IATA DGR (Dangerous Goods Regulations). Failure to follow the packing requirements results in rejection at the airline or return at the destination.

01
Discharge to 30% SOC or below (the core inner-packing rule)
Lithium-ion batteries for air transport must be discharged to a state of charge (SOC) of 30% or below before shipping. Shipping at full charge results in rejection.
SOC limits don't apply to lithium-metal batteries (but discharge/short-circuit prevention is still required) Batteries built into devices must be packed with the device powered off
02
Short-circuit prevention — individual packing or insulation
Each battery must be individually bagged or have its terminals protected with insulating tape. When packing multiple batteries together, they must not touch each other.
Terminal protector caps or insulating tape required Prevent contact between batteries — use individual dividers or packing material
03
Outer packaging — UN-certified containers
PI-grade batteries must use UN-certified outer packaging. PII allows sturdy outer packaging, though UN certification is still recommended.
04
Labels and markings
The outer packaging must display the UN number, the dangerous goods warning label (Class 9), orientation arrows, and the weight.
A separate Lithium Battery Mark (handling label) is required Total package weight must be marked

7. Re-Shipping a Lithium Battery After an EMS Rejection

EMS (international postal service) does not handle lithium batteries as a rule. If your shipment was attempted via EMS and returned, here's how to re-ship it.

🚫 A lithium battery returned by EMS is not simply a "returned item." It must be reclassified as dangerous goods and new documentation prepared. Reusing the original airway bill (AWB) will result in another rejection.
1
Receive and inspect the cargo
Confirm the returned battery's capacity (Wh), quantity, and condition. Also check for damage and the current SOC.
2
Classify as dangerous goods and prepare documentation
Reconfirm the UN number and PI/PII status, and prepare a new MSDS and DGD (Dangerous Goods Declaration). Tosome Logis handles this on your behalf.
3
Discharge SOC and repack
If the state of charge is above 30%, discharge it and repack according to dangerous goods standards.
4
Re-ship via dangerous goods courier or sea freight
Re-ship using DHL/FedEx's dedicated dangerous goods service, or via sea freight forwarding.

9. Frequently Asked Questions About Lithium Battery Export

PI (Section I) and PII (Section II) are distinguished by the battery's Wh capacity. A lithium-ion battery pack is PII at 100Wh or below, and PI above 100Wh up to 160Wh. PII qualifies for relaxed air transport conditions, while PI is subject to stricter quantity and packing limits.
EMS does not handle standalone lithium battery shipments as a rule. Only some small batteries built into devices are allowed in limited cases, and import restrictions vary by country. To ship overseas, use a dangerous-goods-approved courier (DHL/FedEx) or sea freight forwarding.
LiPo drone batteries can be exported by air if they meet the Wh-based PI/PII classification. They must be discharged to 30% SOC or below, packed to prevent short circuits, and comply with quantity limits. For larger capacities or quantities, sea freight is more practical.
For air transport, batteries must be discharged to 30% SOC or below, individually insulated to prevent short circuits, packed in UN-certified outer packaging, and marked with the UN number, warning labels, and weight. Failing to meet these requirements results in rejection at the airline or return at the destination.
