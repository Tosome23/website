Dangerous Goods Air Export From Korea
2026 IATA DGR Rules — MSDS, UN Numbers, and DGD Paperwork

One wrong step in a dangerous goods air export gets your shipment sent back. Unlike general cargo, airlines will only accept a shipment once the UN number, hazard classification, MSDS, and DGD are all in order. Everyday products like lithium batteries, paint, chemicals, and aerosols can all fall under dangerous goods rules. Here's every hazard class, the required documents and packing standards, and the 2026 IATA DGR updates — organized around real processing requirements for exporters shipping from Korea.

1. What Is a Dangerous Goods Export? — UN Numbers and Hazard Classes Explained

Dangerous goods are materials that can cause explosion, fire, toxicity, or corrosion during transport, and IATA (International Air Transport Association) sorts them into 9 hazard classes. Everyday items like lithium batteries, paint, perfume, and aerosols all fall under one of these classes.

A UN number is a four-digit code used to identify dangerous goods internationally. Lithium-ion batteries, for example, are UN3480, and flammable liquids like paint are UN1263. Once the UN number is confirmed, the correct packing, labeling, and documentation standards can be applied.

💡 An MSDS (Material Safety Data Sheet) documents a chemical's composition, physical and chemical properties, hazards, and handling instructions. It's the starting point for any dangerous goods export — the UN number, hazard class, and packing standard are all determined from the MSDS.

2. The 9 Dangerous Goods Hazard Classes (Class 1-9)

Under IATA/IMDG rules, dangerous goods fall into 9 hazard classes below. Air eligibility, packing standards, and quantity limits all vary by class.

4. The 6-Step Dangerous Goods Air Export Process

Dangerous goods air export involves more steps than general cargo. Understanding the 6-step flow below in advance keeps your shipment moving without delay.

01
Submit MSDS and confirm UN number / hazard class
Submit the manufacturer-issued MSDS, and your forwarder will confirm the UN number, hazard class, and packing instruction (PI).
MSDS can be in English or Korean (English preferred) New chemicals without an assigned UN number require prior classification
02
Determine packing standard and prepare UN-certified containers
The Packing Instruction (PI) determines the permitted quantity and container specification. UN-marked certified containers are prepared accordingly.
Primary packaging: a leak-proof sealed container for liquids Outer packaging: UN-certified box or drum Cushioning: absorbent material capable of absorbing at least 30% of the contents is required
03
Prepare DGD and Shipper's Declaration
The Dangerous Goods Declaration (DGD) and shipper confirmation are prepared, and the shipper signs. Tosome Logis handles this on your behalf.
DGD: records the UN number, proper shipping name, hazard class, packing group, quantity, and container type Shipper's Declaration: requires the shipper's signature (obtained separately when we handle it for you)
04
Attach labels and markings
The dangerous goods diamond label, UN number, and orientation arrows (if applicable) must be affixed to the outer packaging in the correct position.
Dangerous goods label: diamond mark per hazard class (100mm × 100mm minimum) UN number marking: displayed on the outer packaging in the format "UN XXXX" Orientation arrow label: required when the contents are liquid
05
Airline acceptance and security screening
The airline inspects the completed documentation and packing condition. If everything checks out, the cargo is accepted.
Certain classes require prior airline approval — 24-72 hours Poor packing condition or incomplete documentation results in immediate rejection

After air dispatch, customs clearance at the destination proceeds based on the dangerous goods declaration.
Same-day intake generally means same-day dispatch (given complete documentation and packing) Arrival in 1-3 days to major US/European cities, 1-2 days to Southeast Asia

5. Dangerous Goods Packing Requirements — UN-Certified Containers and Inner/Outer Packing Standards

Dangerous goods packing is entirely different from general cargo. Airlines will never accept a container without a UN mark.

What Is a UN-Certified Container?

A UN-certified container is one that has passed drop, pressure, and leak tests to UN standards. The container is stamped with the "UN" mark along with the packing group (X/Y/Z), container type code, and maximum permitted weight. Every inner and outer packing layer used for dangerous goods must be a UN-certified container as specified by the applicable PI.

Packing LayerRequirementNotes
Primary (Inner) PackagingLeak-proof seal, suitable UN-certified bottle/tubeGlass, plastic, or metal containers all acceptable (per PI)
Intermediate PackagingProtects the inner packaging; filled with absorbent material (capable of absorbing at least 30% of total liquid volume)Required when the inner packaging is glass
Outer PackagingUN-marked reinforced corrugated board, drum, etc.Must use a container code allowed under the applicable PI
Packing Group (PG)PG I (high hazard), PG II (medium hazard), PG III (low hazard)Found on the MSDS
Maximum QuantityVaries by Section I/II/III under the applicable PIExceeding the limit requires special airline approval

6. Key Changes in the 2026 IATA DGR

The 2026 IATA DGR 67th edition introduced several important changes for dangerous goods air export. Review the following before shipping.

  • Lithium batteries, Section II: Adjustments to permitted quantities and packing standards. Clarified conditions for applying Section II to batteries of 100Wh or below
  • E-cigarettes and vapes: Stricter classification. Separate handling guidelines now apply
  • Updated UN number packing instructions: Several UN number PIs were revised, with clearer packing standards especially for chemical sample categories (UN1993, UN1263)
⚠️ The IATA DGR is revised every year. Checking against the latest edition before shipping is essential. Tosome Logis always processes shipments under the most current DGR rules.

7. Top 5 Most Frequently Rejected Dangerous Goods Types

Here are the item types most often rejected or returned among dangerous goods export requests. Knowing them in advance can shorten your preparation time.

1
Lithium batteries (Class 9 — UN3480/UN3481)
PI 965-970 applies depending on Wh capacity. Section I products require airline pre-approval, and some cannot be loaded on passenger aircraft at all. Failing to meet packing quantity, orientation label, or state-of-charge (30% or below) requirements results in immediate rejection.
2
Paints and coatings (Class 3 — UN1263)
Classified as Class 3 flammable liquid. The packing group is determined by flash point, and requires UN-certified containers and accurate DGD preparation. Declaring it as general cargo under the label "coating" and getting caught results in serious penalties.
3
Perfume and alcohol-based cosmetics (Class 3)
Perfumes, hand sanitizers, and some cosmetics with high alcohol content are classified as flammable liquids. Mistaking them for ordinary consumer goods and packing them normally often results in rejection at the airport. Check the volume threshold (whether the consumer-goods exemption applies) first.
4
Chemicals and lab reagents (Class 3/6/8)
Research reagents and industrial chemicals can fall under Class 3 (flammable), Class 6 (toxic), or Class 8 (corrosive) — sometimes more than one. Without an MSDS, classification isn't even possible. When multiple classes apply, the stricter standard governs.
5
Aerosols (Class 2 — UN1950)
Spray products fall under Class 2 (gas). Additional classification may apply depending on flammability of the contents, and exceeding the volume threshold (e.g. 1L per unit) bans them from passenger aircraft.
