Bought Something From Korea?
Here's the US Customs Duty You'll Owe in 2026

If you ordered something from a Korea-based seller and it's being shipped to a US address by DHL, FedEx, or UPS, customs duty applies no matter how cheap the item was. Since August 29, 2025, the United States eliminated the duty-free allowance that used to exempt packages under $800. For goods made in Korea, the rate you'll see in 2026 is 15%.

🕒 Last updated: August 31, 2026

Why you're being charged duty on a cheap item

Until mid-2025, a package worth $800 or less could enter the US duty-free — this was called the "de minimis" exemption. On July 30, 2025, the White House signed Executive Order 14324, and as of 12:01am ET on August 29, 2025, that exemption was suspended for every country in the world, for shipments arriving through commercial carriers like DHL, FedEx, and UPS[1]. On June 24, 2026, the US Federal Register confirmed this suspension is now treated as indefinite for non-postal shipments, with a statutory full repeal date set for July 1, 2027[2]. In plain terms: if your Korean seller ships your order by courier, duty applies, full stop — even on a $10 accessory.

So how much will you actually owe?

For goods made in Korea, the rate that applies under the US–Korea trade agreement is 15% of the item's declared value — this also covers cars and auto parts. Steel, aluminum, and copper products are taxed separately at up to 50%, regardless of country of origin, and that additional tariff stacks on top of the 15%[3].

If your item is a steel, aluminum, or copper product, or falls under a Section 301/232 trade-remedy list, an extra duty on top of the 15% may apply — this is decided by the item's classification, not by anything the buyer does.

Who actually pays this — you, or the seller?

By default, the buyer (you) is responsible for duty on arrival. But many sellers now ship DDP (Delivered Duty Paid), meaning they prepay the duty on your behalf and build it into the price you were charged at checkout — in that case, you owe nothing extra when the package clears customs. If the seller shipped DDU (Delivered Duty Unpaid) instead, the courier will bill you directly — often through an online payment link — before or at delivery.

💡 If you're not sure which applies to your order, check your order confirmation or ask the seller directly whether duty was included in your purchase price. It's worth asking before you buy, not after the courier contacts you.

What to check before you complain about a customs bill

⚠️ A courier charging you duty on a $20 item isn't a mistake or a scam — it's the current rule for every country shipping into the US, not something specific to your seller. What IS worth checking is the declared value on the shipment: it should match what you actually paid. If it looks wrong, that's a paperwork issue with the seller to sort out, not a reason to refuse the package (which can trigger return shipping costs on top of the duty already assessed).
